Staying up when the cloud went down

How Netflix designed for failure, and kept streaming through a major AWS outage that took many other sites down, plus how to get the same resilience without a chaos-engineering team.

01The challenge

In April 2011 a major outage in Amazon Web Services' US-East region disrupted many well-known sites. Any company running there had to assume that parts of its infrastructure could disappear without warning, and that the question was not whether but when.

02What Netflix did

  • Designed for failure. Services were spread across several availability zones, so losing one did not take the product down.
  • Degraded gracefully. When a dependency failed, the service fell back to a simpler experience instead of failing outright.
  • Broke things on purpose. Chaos Monkey randomly switched off production servers during working hours, so engineers built services that recovered automatically and practised recovery routinely.
  • Learned from the outage. Netflix's own write-up named the gaps the outage exposed, including handling the loss of a whole zone, and fed them back into tooling [1][2].

03The results

Netflix reported that it kept streaming through the April 2011 outage without a major customer-facing outage, while it acknowledged where its systems needed to improve [1]. Chaos Monkey was later open-sourced (2012), and the practice it started became known as chaos engineering.

The lesson: reliability comes from rehearsal. Systems that are regularly made to fail, on your schedule, recover when failure arrives on its own.

04At your scale: a 90-day plan

You don't need a chaos-engineering team. A handful of deliberate practices give a mid-size product most of the resilience.

Days 1–30 · Define "up"Days 31–60 · Remove single pointsDays 61–90 · Rehearse
  • Pick the customer journey that pays the bills (sign-up, checkout, login)
  • Set an SLO for it, e.g. 99.9% successful requests
  • Alert on SLO burn, not on every CPU spike
  • Run across two or more availability zones behind a load balancer
  • Health checks and autoscaling on every service
  • Managed databases with automated failover and tested backups
  • First game day: switch off one component on purpose, in staging
  • Write runbooks for what you learned
  • Repeat quarterly, then carefully in production

05Readiness checklist

  • You have a written SLO for your key journey
  • Losing one server doesn't page anyone
  • Losing one zone doesn't take you down
  • Backups have been restored, not just taken
  • Alerts fire on customer impact, not noise
  • There's an on-call rota and runbooks
  • Incidents get a blameless review
  • You've switched something off on purpose this quarter
